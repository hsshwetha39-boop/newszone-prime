Karnataka CM DK Shivakumar and Daughter Aishwarya Face Backlash Over Remarks on Government School Teachers

By Shwetha • November 9, 2026 • 3 min read

Karnataka Chief Minister D.K. Shivakumar and his daughter, Aishwarya DKS Hegde, have drawn criticism from teachers' representatives, educationists and student groups over comments about the work of government school teachers. The row has also sharpened opposition to a state plan that invites private institutions and companies to adopt government schools.

How the Row Began

On November 4, Shivakumar appealed to private school managements to adopt government schools in rural areas or partner with the state in developing them. According to PTI, he suggested that private schools could send subject experts to government schools and pay their salaries, provide laboratory equipment and computers, and hand over buses to government school students instead of selling them. He added that institutions supporting a school would be allowed to add their own name to it.

Reports mention a target of linking between 1,000 and 2,000 government schools with private institutions. The government has presented the plan as a way to improve the quality of rural education and reduce migration from villages to cities.

Speaking about teacher pay, the Chief Minister said government school teachers earn between about ₹70,000 and ₹1.5 lakh a month. Later reports said he voiced disappointment about the commitment of some teachers drawing around ₹1.5 lakh, but clarified that he was not criticising the whole teaching community.

Aishwarya Hegde's Remarks

Aishwarya Hegde, trustee-secretary of the National Education Foundation, spoke at a corporate social responsibility (CSR) event organised by the state government. Her foundation supports the Karnataka Public School in Doddalahalli, and she used it as an example while discussing teacher accountability.

"More than teacher shortage, low efficiency is the problem," she said. She claimed that some teachers earning around ₹1.1 lakh were not coming to school regularly, and argued that organisations investing in government schools should be able to check attendance and performance.

Teachers' Representatives and Educationists Respond

A.P. Ranganath, a Janata Dal (Secular) MLC for the Bangalore Teachers' Constituency, said the comments undermined the dignity of government school teachers, who are recruited through eligibility tests and competitive selection. He pointed out that teachers also handle many tasks outside the classroom, including supervising midday meals, distributing eggs, and taking part in census, caste and livestock surveys, electoral roll revision and election duty. Together with vacant posts and inadequate infrastructure, he said, these duties put heavy pressure on staff.

Ranganath argued that private and government schools should not be judged by the same standards. "Private education may be a choice, but public education is a constitutional and social responsibility," he said. He asked Aishwarya to apologise to teachers and cited Articles 21A and 14 of the Constitution. Reports differ on whether his criticism was aimed only at her or at both her and her father.

The Save Public Education Committee accused the government of shifting the blame for problems in public education onto teachers. It called the adoption plan "anti-people" and objected to private institutions attaching their names to adopted schools. A meeting organised by the committee drew former vice-chancellors A. Murigeppa and Sabiha Bhoomigowda, writers K. Marulasiddappa, S.G. Siddaramaiah, Roopa Hassan and Hema Pattanshetty, and educationist V.P. Niranjanaradhya.

The All-India Democratic Students' Organisation demanded that the policy be withdrawn. The RTE Students Parents Association said that making sure government schools function properly is the state's responsibility, and the All Karnataka Pre-University Lecturers' Association president, A.H. Ningegowda, called for vacant teaching posts to be filled. Some commentators have described the plan as "backdoor privatisation", while the government presents it as a partnership.

What the Debate Is About

The dispute brings together several questions: how to ensure teacher accountability, how to fill vacancies, how to improve infrastructure, how many non-teaching duties teachers should carry, and what role private institutions and CSR money should play in public education. Supporters of the plan see extra resources and expertise for rural schools. Critics say voluntary contributions cannot replace the state's duty to run a well-staffed public school system.

Sources: reports by Daily Pioneer, PTI (via The Week), Devdiscourse, Education Post and The Federal.
